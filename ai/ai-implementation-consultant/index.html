AI implementation consultant: build from the process

As an AI implementation consultant, I turn a defined business process into a working system you can inspect. I start with the work, the people, and the failure points. Then I build a limited workflow, test its behavior, and give you control over what it can do.

Is this for a business that already knows what needs fixing?

You may already know where work gets stuck. A request arrives, someone copies it into another screen, and the next person waits for a detail nobody collected. You want that handoff repaired. You do not need another screen full of moving charts.

I build and test AI systems for Santa Clarita Valley and Los Angeles County businesses. This service fits an owner who can name a recurring process and provide someone who understands it. If you are still deciding which problem deserves attention, my small business AI consulting page describes that earlier decision.

What do you inspect before choosing any tool?

I ask you to walk me through a request from arrival to completion. Show me the form, the inbox, the notes, and the place where the work finally lands. Use a sample with private details removed. I want to see what people actually do, including the sticky note beside the keyboard.

Then I map the source of each fact, who can change it, and who approves the next action. I check the tools you already have before recommending another. If a required field or a clearer assignment solves the problem, that is the change I propose.

How do we decide what the system is allowed to do?

We write the boundaries before I connect anything. A system might read an approved request, draft a summary, and place it in a review queue. That does not give it permission to send a message, change a booking, or overwrite a customer record.

For each action, I name the trigger, required information, destination, and approving person. Missing information gets a defined response. I also record what counts as success in the receiving system. A draft appearing on a screen is different from the correct employee receiving a usable task. The scope needs to say which outcome we are testing.

What would a useful implementation look like?

Picture an office in Valencia handling service requests while the owner is out on appointments. This is an example, not a client result. Staff need a clear description of the request, the contact route the customer supplied, and a person assigned to respond.

I might build a workflow that organizes those details and flags omissions. A fixed rule can assign a known category. AI might help turn a long message into a readable summary. Staff compare that summary with the original before relying on it. The goal is a better handoff, with the source still available when someone has a question.

What will you refuse to automate?

I will not let a generated answer invent a business policy, authorize a payment, or make a commitment that belongs to you. I will not build a workflow that hides its mistakes by deleting the source. I also refuse to treat access to an account as permission to use every feature inside it.

Sensitive decisions stay with the appropriate person. An upset customer needs a reachable human, not a loop of confident replies. When the business has no approved answer, the system must ask for help. We can automate preparation around a decision without handing the decision itself to software.

How do you test before anything reaches a customer?

I begin with sample records in a separated test route. I check ordinary requests, missing details, conflicting instructions, repeated submissions, and messages that try to redirect the system. The expected response is written before the test runs. That keeps a convincing answer from being mistaken for a correct answer.

I trace the result into its destination. Did the right record change? Was the responsible employee notified? Could the same request create duplicate work? I also interrupt a connection and check recovery. My article on AI checklists and missing dependencies is related reading for this part of the work.

Who decides when the workflow can go live?

You approve the release boundaries after reviewing the evidence. I show the intended behavior, the failures found, and the remaining limitations. We identify who watches the initial live work and how they stop it. A successful demonstration does not replace that decision.

The release should begin with the smallest useful scope. I keep a manual route available and document how to pause the automated route without losing incoming work. If a test fails after a change, I retest the affected path. New instructions, changed permissions, and updated source material can all alter behavior that previously passed.

What do I keep control of after the build?

You retain the business decisions: approved content, account permissions, sending authority, and whether the workflow stays active. We identify which accounts belong to your business and who administers them. Where a platform limits portability, I call that out before it becomes part of the design.

The handoff includes the process map, operating instructions, test examples, and a named person responsible for maintenance. I explain how to update the approved information and how to recognize a failure. If your staff cannot tell what happened or who should respond, the implementation needs more work before it becomes part of the day.

How should I prepare for the first conversation?

Bring a process you can describe without opening a private customer file. Explain what starts it, where it slows down, and what a good result looks like. A redacted example is more useful than a list of products you have heard about. Keep passwords and access codes out of the conversation.

You can review the broader approach on my AI page. The AI strategy call explains how we narrow the first discussion. I would rather leave that conversation with a clear boundary and a practical test than a large build nobody can supervise.

What else should I know before we begin?

Do I need to replace my existing software?

I inspect the current tools and process first. If an existing feature can handle the work reliably, I include that option. A replacement needs a specific reason, including how records and staff routines would move.

Can you implement a workflow someone else planned?

I can review the proposed workflow against the actual business process. I still need to inspect its permissions, assumptions, and failure handling before treating the plan as ready to build.

Will the AI send messages without my approval?

Sending authority is an explicit scope decision. I start with a review path where needed and require approval for any automatic sending behavior. A draft workflow does not silently become a sending workflow.

What happens if the test fails?

I identify the failed condition and correct the workflow or reduce its scope. I repeat the relevant checks before recommending release. An unresolved failure stays visible in the handoff.

Who handles changes after launch?

We name the maintenance owner and define the review process before release. Updates to business rules, source information, or connected accounts need an accountable person and another check of the affected behavior.

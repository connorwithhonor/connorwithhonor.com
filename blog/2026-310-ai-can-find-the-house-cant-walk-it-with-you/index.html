CWH-2026-310 // The Machine + The House

AI Can Find the House. It Can't Walk It With You.

November 6, 2026 // Daily Download // Connor MacIvor
TL;DRAI is getting very good at the search. Lists, sales history, patterns, who might sell next. That part of the job is close to solved. What it cannot do yet is stand in a hallway with you, notice what you are too excited to see, and tell you about it before it costs you money. On my November 3 show I talked to agents about AI. This is the version for the rest of us: the people who hire agents, contractors, advisors and anyone else whose job AI is supposed to be taking. The machine finds the house. You still want a human to walk it with you. Here is how to pick the right one.

The fear everybody is selling

You have heard the line. Real estate agents are going to be replaced hand over fist by AI. Swap in any job you like. The story is always told with a little too much enthusiasm.

I recorded a show about this on November 3, talking mostly to my fellow agents. My view: it is partially true. Some of the work really is going to the machine. But the part that matters most when you are spending real money is not the part AI is good at.

What AI really does well

Let us give the machine its due. It is a monster at the search.

On the show I described a job I have done by hand for years. You take the lists you can get from title companies, the sales history for a neighborhood, how long people have owned their homes. You load it in. Then you let AI look for the pattern: who is most likely to sell next, what those homes might bring, where the activity is moving.

That used to take an afternoon and a pot of coffee. Now it takes minutes. The agent with better data and a good AI process finds the likely sellers first. That is real. I teach it.

Same goes for you. AI can compare neighborhoods, read a long document, or explain a term you have never heard. Use it. I do, every day.

The search is now table stakes.

What AI cannot do: walk the house with you

Here is the other half.

Back when I still represented buyers, before I went sellers only as a Sellers Only Agent™, the most important part of the job happened after we pulled up to the curb. I was never the guy who walked in and said, look at this glorious room, can you not just picture your family here. That is the easy stuff. The buyer is already picturing it.

I was looking at the roof.

On the show I put it this way: "Looks like it's got at least two or three layers on it." Then I would tell the client that if we ever got to an accepted offer, I wanted the inspector to pay particular attention up there, and here is how you can tell.

And then the important admission: "I don't know how to replace a roof." I am not a roofer. I am a guy who has been looking at houses since 1998 and has learned to make plain judgments and hand them to the client, so at least they are thinking about it.

That is the job AI cannot do from a server farm. It cannot smell the water damage. It cannot see a buyer's face go bright and know that is the exact moment they stop noticing anything.

"A human being wants to walk a property with another human being, not a robot."

Your guide's job is not to make you fall in love

This is the part I want every regular person to hear, because it applies far beyond real estate.

The best people you will ever hire are not the cheerleaders. They are the ones who point out what is wrong.

Here is how I said it on November 3: "my job isn't to have them fall in love with anything. My job is to point out the facts." A buyer who loves a house and does not know what they are buying is a buyer who finds out three weeks into the process. That hurts them. It hurts the seller too, because the seller just lost three weeks with somebody who was never going to close.

I learned that habit wearing a badge. Twenty years with the LAPD teaches you to scan a room for what is off before you look at what is pretty. On the show I said my clients like me to "point out the danger signs just like I used to do when I was a cop." Same skill. Different uniform.

So when you interview anybody, an agent, a financial planner, a contractor, a doctor's office, listen for one thing. Do they tell you what could go wrong? Or do they only tell you what you want to hear? A machine will happily do the second one all day. It is built to be agreeable. You need at least one human in the room whose job is the first one.

AI is a toddler. We have mileage.

On the show I said, "AI hasn't been around that long. They're all a whole bunch of toddlers at this point."

That is not an insult. Toddlers learn fast, and I would not bet against them. But a toddler has not buried a parent. A toddler has not lost a job, sat in a waiting room with a diagnosis, or been burned by somebody they trusted.

People have. When you make one of the biggest decisions of your life, part of what you are hiring is that mileage. Maybe they got taken advantage of once and never forgot it. That shared history is why you trust their read.

I am 57. A lot of miles on the odometer, and some of them were rough. That is what a person brings that a model, for now, does not.

How to hire the human in the AI era

Here is the practical part. Five questions I would ask anybody I was about to trust with real money, in any field.

1. How do you use AI in your work?

You want a real answer. "It helps me pull and sort the data, then I check it" is good. "Oh, I do not touch that stuff" is a yellow flag in 2026. "AI does everything for me" is a red one. And it cuts both ways: you can use AI to fact-check the pros, too. I welcome it, and I said so in they're using AI to catch me lying, good, bring it.

2. What is something you would warn me about right now?

A good guide names a risk in the first five minutes. If they cannot, they are selling, not guiding.

3. What will you check in person that a computer cannot?

For a house, it is the roof, the walls, the smell, the neighbors. For a business deal, it is the people. Make them say it out loud.

4. What happens when something goes quiet?

Deals stall. On the show I talked about a buyer going dark after an inspection. A good human picks up the phone instead of waiting and hoping. Ask how they handle silence.

5. Will you push back on me?

The best thing a human can do is disagree with you before it gets expensive. I tell people to do the same thing with their AI, too. Make it argue. I wrote about that habit in always push back on AI before it embarrasses you.

A word to the professionals reading this

If you are an agent, or any service pro worried about the machine: my message on the show was simple. "So don't abandon your humanness." Do not panic. Dig in. Use AI for the search, then go be the person who walks the house.

That is the point of keeping the skill under the AI.

Where this leaves us

The machine is going to take a lot of the searching. Good. Let it.

What it leaves behind is the part that was always the real job. Noticing. Warning. Picking up the phone. Telling somebody the truth about the roof when they would rather hear about the kitchen.

So use AI to find the house. Then bring a human who has been around the block to walk it with you. That pairing beats either one alone.

Questions people are asking

Will AI replace real estate agents?

In Connor's view, AI will take over much of the searching and data work. It is not close to replacing the person who walks the property, spots problems and guides the client.

What can AI do well in real estate today?

The search: sorting lists, comparing sales history and flagging who might sell next. Connor uses it for exactly that, with a human checking the output.

What should I look for in an agent or advisor in the AI era?

Look for someone who uses AI for the grunt work and still tells you what could go wrong. Ask them to name a risk in the first five minutes, explain what they check in person, and say how they handle a deal that goes quiet.

Why does Connor call AI a toddler?

Because it is new. It learns fast, but it has no life experience. A person who has been through job loss, illness or a bad deal brings judgment a model does not have yet.

Should I use AI myself when buying or selling a home?

Use it to learn: compare neighborhoods, decode terms, draft your questions. Then take those questions to a licensed professional who will walk the property with you. AI is a study partner, not a stand-in for your representation.

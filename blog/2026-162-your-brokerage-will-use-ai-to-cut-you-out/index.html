Your Brokerage Will Use AI To Cut You Out

September 20, 2026 // Daily Download // Connor MacIvor
TL;DR Bill Gates says jobs are going to change, and real estate is part of that conversation, and it should be. AI is not coming for the real estate agent. It is coming for the parts of the job that are pure repetition, and the brokerages, portals, and syndication sites will automate those parts the second it saves them money, because that is what every business does when technology creates leverage. There are three tiers to this job. Tier one, repetitive communication. Tier two, data and workflow. Both get automated, and both should. Tier three is judgment: reading a scared or grieving person, knowing when to push and when to shut up, owning the consequences of the advice. A 1966 line from Michael Polanyi explains it better than most AI commentary does: we know more than we can tell. That is the tier a machine cannot touch yet. Automate the first two. Get dangerous at the third. That is the whole game.

Bill Gates says a lot of jobs are going to change. Real estate keeps ending up in those conversations, and it should. There are parts of this business that can absolutely be automated. I sell some of that automation. I built HonorElevate. I built HireAIVoice. I use AI in my own real estate business every day, the way I broke down in your real estate agent uses AI, mine builds it. So I am not going to stand here and tell you AI cannot touch real estate. It already has.

The question is not whether AI touches your business. The question is who gets replaced first. Is it the agent who learns how to use the tools, or the agent who keeps doing everything manually while Zillow, Realtor.com, the brokerages, and every big platform in this business figure out how to keep the money moving without needing as many of us in the middle?

Why Would Your Own Brokerage Use AI Against You?

Let us stop pretending these companies are our friends. They are not, but they are not evil either. They are businesses. They love agents when agents bring in revenue. They love us when we pay our cap, buy leads, buy ads, buy software, buy signs, buy that next shiny tool. But if they can use AI to answer the phone, qualify the lead, nurture it, market the property, handle the paperwork, schedule the showing, and push the transaction forward while keeping the same money coming in, you think they would not do that in a New York second? Of course they would. They would call it innovation. They would put out a press release. If they were publicly traded, their stock would go up.

They are asking how much of your production is the human, and how much is the system.

That is not paranoia. That is how every industry behaves when technology creates leverage. The companies patting agents on the back for closing 50 or 100 deals a year are also looking at the numbers, asking how much of that production is the human and how much can be turned into a workflow, a system, an AI assistant that works every hour of every day, never needs a vacation, and never says I'll call tomorrow. That part is real.

What Can AI Actually Automate In A Real Estate Business?

There is another part nobody explains correctly. AI does not replace a real estate agent just because it can read a script or pull a listing off Zillow. For AI to actually do the work, it needs access to the work. It needs the CRM, the call history, the text messages, the disclosures, the transaction timeline. It needs to know whether the seller is just curious, getting divorced, behind on payments, handling an estate, or racing a court date. That is not sitting in the MLS remarks. It is in the relationship, in the file, in the conversation that happened before someone trusted you enough to tell you the truth.

That is the difference. AI can work from the information you give it and the systems you connect it to. It can automate the repeatable parts. It does not magically understand a family selling their mother's house because it scraped a few websites. Today there is a canyon between it can answer the phone and it can carry the weight of the decision. Those are not the same job, and this is where I break the work into three tiers.

Tier One: Repetitive Communication

Answering the phone. Responding to a lead. Confirming an appointment. Texting someone back at 8:42 p.m. because they finally got around to asking about the house. This should be automated, not because people do not matter, but because a slow response kills deals. If your phone rings and nobody answers, the lead does not wait patiently. They call somebody else. This is exactly why HonorElevate exists, not to pretend the machine is the agent, but to stop the agent from losing business over work that should have been handled in the first place.

Tier Two: Data And Workflow

Pulling property information. Building a first draft of a CMA. Flagging missing paperwork. Checking deadlines. Assembling disclosures. Tracking who has signed and who has not. Automate it. A system does this faster, cleaner, and with fewer mistakes than you can. I do it all myself is not a badge of honor. Usually it is just a bad process wearing a costume. You are not getting paid to copy and paste information between two screens. You are getting paid for judgment and accountability, for protecting someone's equity when the stakes are real.

Tier Three: Where The Work Gets Human

Telling a seller their home is not worth what they hoped. Explaining why the offer that hurts their feelings may be the right offer. Knowing when a buyer's agent is bluffing, and when they are not. Knowing when to push and when to shut up. Knowing when the person across from you is overwhelmed, scared, angry, grieving, broke, or trying to hold their life together while they sell a house. AI can write a beautiful email. AI cannot sit across from somebody and own the consequences of that advice. Not today.

What Did Michael Polanyi Mean By "We Know More Than We Can Tell"?

A man named Michael Polanyi wrote something in 1966 that explains this better than most AI commentary does. He said we know more than we can tell. We know more than we can write down, more than we can put into a checklist. A good negotiator cannot always explain why they pushed when they did. I saw that a lot of times with LAPD negotiators, and you have seen it in the movies. An inkling, an internal cue you cannot extrapolate or explain. A good agent cannot always explain why a seller is saying yes when they mean no. You can look at the words, the numbers, the data, but the human being sitting in front of you is not a spreadsheet. That does not make AI useless. It means we need to be clear about which part of the job we are actually handing off.

What Happened When Zillow Tried To Automate The Whole Transaction?

Zillow proved part of this years ago. They had more data than most people in this business will ever touch. Engineers, money, massive consumer traffic. Zillow Offers tried to turn home buying into an algorithmic machine: price it, buy it, resell it, scale it. It did not work. They shut it down after losing hundreds of millions of dollars. That does not mean data is worthless or AI is a joke. It means real estate is messy. A house is not just square footage and a lot size on a graph. There is a family inside that decision. A street that feels different from the next street over. A buyer who falls in love with something the spreadsheet does not understand. A seller who takes less money because certainty matters more than squeezing out another fifteen thousand dollars. The data matters. It just does not carry the entire load.

What Should An Agent Actually Do About This?

If your business is built entirely on tier one and tier two work, you are exposed right now, not in ten years. If your value is answering the phone, sending the same follow-up, copying data into systems, scheduling showings, and hoping people remember you called them back, somebody with better systems is going to beat you. Maybe another agent. Maybe a brokerage. Maybe a portal. Maybe your own brokerage. A team leader with one good AI stack and ten agents underneath can outrun you, and it is coming.

You do not wait for Zillow or your brokerage to hand you the system built to replace half of what you do. I made the same case to SRAR in Dig in Like an Alabama Tick. Build your own system now. Own your own database, your own relationships, your own follow-up, your own brand, your own websites, your own content. Own the part of the business a policy change or an algorithm update cannot take from you. If all you own is your MLS login and a lead source somebody else controls, you do not own much. You are renting your career from a platform, and that is not a business. That is a month to month lease.

Using AI does not mean handing AI the keys. I do not use it because I want to disappear from my business. I use it because I want to spend less time on work that does not need me, so the system watches the ball while I do the work only I can do. Talking to people. Doing videos like this. Giving the hard answers. Negotiating. Protecting the seller. Getting the highest possible price in the shortest possible time with the least amount of nonsense. That is still the work.

The Bottom Line

The agent of the future is not the one who refuses AI. That agent gets buried under their own busy work. The agent of the future is also not the one who thinks AI will do everything. That agent becomes a button pusher for somebody else's platform. The agent who survives automates the repeatable work and gets better at the human work: trust, communication, negotiation, reading people, telling the truth when it costs them a listing. That is where the money will be. Not in typing the same follow-up for the four hundredth time.

The rich companies are using AI to get richer. Good. We can use it too. We can answer the phone. We can build tighter systems. We can stop donating leads to whoever simply got back to the client first. These platforms will replace you if they can, in a heartbeat. That is not personal. That is math. So the move is not fear. The move is ownership. Automate tier one. Automate tier two. Get dangerous at tier three. That is the whole game. Let's be careful out there. I'm Connor with honor, and I'll see you in the next one.

FAQ

Will AI replace real estate agents?

Not the agent. The parts of the job that are pure repetition. AI cannot yet sit across from a grieving family and own the consequences of the advice it gives them, but it can already answer the phone, qualify a lead, nurture a follow-up, and assemble a CMA faster and cheaper than a human can. The agents at risk are the ones whose entire value was doing that repeatable work manually. The ones who automate the repeatable work and get better at the human work are the ones who survive.

Why would a brokerage or portal want to reduce the number of agents?

Brokerages, portals, and syndication sites make money keeping the transaction moving and the customer on their platform. They are not evil, they are businesses, and they love agents when agents generate revenue and pay fees. But if AI can answer the phone, qualify the lead, market the property, and push the paperwork forward while keeping the same money flowing, a public company would adopt that in a heartbeat and call it innovation. That is not paranoia, it is how every industry behaves when new technology creates leverage.

What are the three tiers of a real estate agent's job?

Tier one is repetitive communication: answering the phone, confirming appointments, following up after an open house. Tier two is data and workflow: pulling property information, building a first-draft CMA, tracking disclosures and deadlines. Tier three is judgment: telling a seller their home is not worth what they hoped, knowing when a buyer's agent is bluffing, carrying the emotional weight of a family's decision. AI is already strong at tiers one and two. Tier three is where the agent still is the product.

What did Michael Polanyi mean by "we know more than we can tell"?

Polanyi, writing in 1966, described tacit knowledge: the expertise a skilled person uses without being able to fully explain it. A negotiator who senses the right moment to push cannot always write out why. A good agent who reads that a seller's yes actually means no is working from the same kind of knowledge. AI can process data and language, but it cannot yet replicate the tacit, felt judgment that comes from sitting across from real people in real, high-stakes moments.

AI Writing Still Needs Your Voice and Your Judgment

Connor MacIvor · October 4, 2026 · From the AI with Honor LIVE

A draft that sounds confident still needs facts, useful links and your judgment.

Watch the Matching LIVE Excerpt

In my second October 4 AI with Honor LIVE I talked about wanting useful links and writing that sounds like me. That want is simple to say and hard to get. An AI tool can produce a long draft quickly. What you cannot get back, unless you build it in yourself, is your voice, your facts, and your judgment about what a real reader actually needs. If you own a business and you publish articles on your own website, the process below is how you keep the speed of the machine without publishing something that reads like it came from a vending machine.

This is written for a business owner creating useful original articles on a site they own. Every example below is hypothetical. None of them describe real clients, and none of them promise a result. The work is the work. Here is how to do it.

Start With a Question a Real Reader Is Asking

Every worthwhile article answers a question someone actually has. Not a question a keyword tool invented. A question a person asked you with their own mouth, or typed into your contact form, or raised on a phone call twice in the same week.

Picture a hypothetical dog groomer. Three customers in one month ask why their doodle's coat mats so fast between appointments. That is an article. The groomer already knows the answer, already knows the follow-up questions, and already knows the mistakes owners make at home. An AI tool can help organize that answer into clean paragraphs. The tool does not establish what this groomer’s customers actually asked. That context needs to come from the business.

Before you open any writing tool, write the question down in the words the customer used. Not your polished version. Theirs. A hypothetical HVAC owner might hear "why does my upstairs never cool down" instead of "zoning inefficiencies in two-story homes." The customer's phrasing is the one other people with the same problem will recognize.

Then write one sentence describing who is asking and what they want to be able to do after reading. "A homeowner with a two-story house wants to know whether the fix is a setting, a filter, or a service call." That sentence becomes your north star for everything else. If a paragraph in the finished draft does not help that person, it goes.

Gather Source Material You Actually Own

The second step is collecting raw material, and the rule is strict. Use material you own or material you have verified yourself. Your own explanations, approved notes, original photos you have permission to use, and answers to recurring questions. Owning a document does not automatically authorize sharing every detail inside it. Keep customer identities, confidential terms and other protected material out of an unapproved tool. Use an approved summary or invented example when that is sufficient.

A hypothetical pool service owner sits down to write about winterizing. Instead of asking an AI tool to explain winterizing from scratch, he searches his own sent mail for the word "winterize" and finds eleven replies he wrote to customers over three seasons. Those replies help him identify his process and the questions he has already answered. He prepares a summary without customer details before putting any material into an approved drafting tool.

What you do not do is feed the tool somebody else's article and ask for a reworded version. A paraphrase with the sentences shuffled is still somebody else's thinking. It adds nothing, it teaches your reader nothing new, and it is not yours. If you read widely to learn, good. Then close the tabs and write from what you now know and can verify, in your own structure, with your own examples.

Keep the research record with the production materials. Give the reader an original, complete explanation, and preserve any attribution or credit that the material requires. Rephrasing another article does not make its work yours.

Separate Your Voice From Your Claims

Before drafting, split your material into two piles. One pile is claims, statements that are either true or false. The other pile is voice, the way you say things.

A claim: "We answer the phone during business hours, and after hours a call service takes a message." That is checkable. A voice element: the way a hypothetical electrician always says "the panel tells the story" when he explains diagnostics. That is not a fact to verify. It is a fingerprint.

The reason this split matters is that AI tools can fail in different ways. On claims, a tool can confidently invent. It will give your business hours it made up, describe a warranty you do not offer, or attribute a certification you never earned. On voice, a tool can flatten the writing and replace your fingerprint phrases with the same smooth filler it gives everyone else.

So you treat the piles differently. Claims get locked down in writing before drafting starts, and nothing in the draft is allowed to contradict or extend them. Voice gets captured as a short set of examples and patterns, and the draft gets measured against them during editing.

The hypothetical electrician writes his claims on one page: licensed in his state, serving three named towns, panel upgrades and troubleshooting as core services. On another page he lists five sentences he has actually said to customers. Keeping the two records separate makes it easier to notice an unsupported claim hiding inside a polished sentence.

Build a Short Voice Brief Before You Prompt Anything

A voice brief is one page that describes how you sound. Not how you wish you sounded. How you actually talk when you are explaining something to a customer at the counter.

A hypothetical bakery owner builds hers like this. She records herself answering a customer question about custom cake lead times, then reads the transcript. She notices she uses short sentences. She notices she always gives the reason behind a rule, not just the rule. She notices she says "here's the thing" when she gets to the important part. She writes down those three observations, adds four phrases she really uses, and adds three things she would never say, including "artisanal" and "curated," words that appear on every competitor's site and never once in her mouth.

That is the whole brief. Patterns, real phrases, banned words. Keep it under a page so you will actually use it.

The brief does two jobs. First, it shapes the prompt. When you ask a tool to draft a section, you include the brief so the first pass starts closer to you. Second, and more important, it shapes the edit. When you read the draft, every sentence gets held against the brief. Does this sound like something I would say out loud to a customer? If you cannot imagine the words leaving your mouth, rewrite them until you can.

Update the brief as you catch new patterns. Revise it when an article reveals a useful correction. Check whether the updated brief improves the next draft rather than assuming it will.

Take Inventory of Your Facts

Open a blank document and build a facts inventory for the article before any drafting happens. This is the list of every specific, checkable detail the article will touch.

A hypothetical bookkeeper writing about year-end preparation lists hers: the software platforms she actually supports, the document categories she asks clients to gather, the months her calendar fills up, the services she offers and the ones she refers out, and the correct spelling of her own firm name, which gets mangled constantly. She pulls each item from the authoritative source, her engagement letter, her own service page, her calendar, not from memory.

The inventory earns its keep twice. During drafting, it is the only approved source of specifics. If a detail is not on the list, the draft does not get to state it. During editing, it is the answer key. You read the draft with the inventory beside it and check every specific against the list.

The common failure here is the missing dependency, the fact you assumed you had but never actually captured. The draft needs your founding year and you realize you are not sure if it was the year you registered or the year you took your first client. The draft references a service you quietly stopped offering last spring. I wrote about this failure pattern in a piece on what happens when an AI checklist is missing its dependencies, and the fix is the same here. Surface every needed fact before the work starts, not during it.

Outline Before You Generate

An outline gives your judgment a place to shape the article before drafting begins. Skip it and the AI tool will impose its own default structure, which may be generic or poorly suited to this reader’s question.

A hypothetical landscaper writing about fall cleanup builds his outline around the order a homeowner actually experiences the problem. First the question of timing, because that is the first thing people ask. Then what a cleanup actually includes, because most homeowners picture raking and nothing else. Then what happens to lawns that skip it, because that is the stakes. Then what to look for when hiring anyone, including his competitors, because that is the section that proves he is not just selling.

Notice what drove that structure. Not a template. His experience of how the conversation actually goes in a driveway.

Under each heading, write two or three bullet points pulled from your facts inventory and your source material. These bullets are instructions to your future self and to the tool. They say what the section covers, which means they also say what it does not cover.

Then read the outline top to bottom and ask one question. If my reader only reads the headings, do they still get the shape of the answer? If yes, you have an outline. If the headings are vague labels like "Overview" and "Benefits," you have a template, and templates are where voice goes to die.

Draft in Sections, Not in One Shot

Now you draft, and the method matters. For an article that needs careful review, try drafting one section at a time. A full-length single pass can drift. The middle sections go soft, examples repeat, and by the end important constraints may be missing from the result.

Work one section at a time. Give the tool the voice brief, the relevant facts from your inventory, the bullets for that section alone, and a clear instruction about who the reader is. Then read what comes back immediately, while your intent for that section is still fresh.

A hypothetical auto shop owner drafting a piece on timing belt replacement works this way. For the section on warning signs, he feeds in the three symptoms he actually sees in his bays, described the way he describes them to customers. The tool returns clean paragraphs, but it also adds a fourth symptom he has never once encountered. He cuts it on the spot. It did not come from his inventory, so it does not survive, no matter how plausible it sounds. Plausible is the most dangerous word in AI drafting. The tool is built to sound right. Sounding right and being right are different jobs, and the second one is yours.

As you accept each section, do a quick voice pass before moving on. Swap flat phrasing for how you would say it. Fixing voice section by section beats fixing a whole flattened article at the end, the same way sweeping a shop nightly beats shoveling it out once a year.

Check Every Claim Like It Will Be Challenged

With the full draft assembled, the audit begins. Read the article line by line and mark every statement of fact. Then verify each one against a source you control or can check yourself.

The standard to hold is simple. Imagine a skeptical customer calling you and reading a sentence back to you over the phone. Can you back it up? A hypothetical moving company owner runs this audit on her draft about long-distance moves. The draft says her crews are background checked. She checks the actual completed records and the scope of the checks; a vendor agreement alone would not establish that every crew member was checked. The draft says most moves of a certain distance take a set number of days. She did not write that, the tool did, and she has no source for it. It gets cut and replaced with what she actually knows, the range her own jobs have run, stated as her experience and not as an industry fact.

Three categories need extra teeth. Statistics, because the tool invents them fluently. Legal and medical statements, because being wrong there carries real consequences and those calls belong to licensed professionals, not your blog. And claims about other businesses, because you rarely know a competitor's operation as well as you think.

One more rule. Do not assume your AI tool verified anything, including links, unless your actual workflow gives it that ability and you have seen it work. A model that cannot open a webpage may still describe it confidently. You check sources yourself, in your own browser, every time.

Link to Your Own Pages Where It Helps the Reader

Internal links were half of what I was after in that LIVE, and the standard for them is one word: useful. A link earns its place when the reader, at that exact sentence, might reasonably want to go deeper on that exact thing, and you have a page that delivers.

A hypothetical plumber writing about water heater lifespan mentions his inspection service in passing. That mention is the natural place to link his inspection page, with the anchor text describing what the reader will find, not "click here" and not a keyword brick stuffed in for a machine's benefit. If he has an older article on sediment flushing, the paragraph about maintenance is where that link lives. Each link answers a question the reader just formed.

What kills internal linking is linking for your own sake. Ten links jammed into a short post, pointing at pages with only a passing connection to the sentence they sit in, teach the reader that your links waste their time. Three links that each pay off teach the reader to trust the next one.

The same clarity matters when considering how an AI-assisted search tool might interpret your pages. Clear, well-linked explanations of what you do are part of how AI agents help people find and understand a business in the first place. And after you place every link, open each one in your browser and confirm it lands where you intended. A dead or wrong link is a small broken promise, and small broken promises add up.

Cut the Repetition and the Generic Filler

Now the hard edit. Read the full draft hunting for two specific diseases: repetition and generic language. AI drafts carry both.

Repetition hides in restated ideas, not copied sentences. The tool loves to end a section by summarizing the section, then open the next section by summarizing it again. A hypothetical florist editing her piece on wedding flower planning finds the phrase about booking early, in different costumes, in four separate sections. One stays, the strongest one. Three go.

Generic language is easier to spot once you know the tells. "It is important to note." "When it comes to." Any sentence that would be equally true on a competitor's website is a sentence that says nothing about you. The test is substitution. Paste a rival's business name into the sentence. If it still reads fine, check whether the sentence provides useful information or merely generic praise. A clear explanation of a common process can still help a reader. The florist's draft says she is "passionate about making your day special." Every florist in the county could run that line. She replaces it with what she actually does, building each proposal around three flowers the couple names in their first meeting. One of those is information. The other is wallpaper.

Vague quantities get the same treatment. "Fast turnaround" becomes the actual timeframe you quote customers. "Years of experience" becomes the number. Specific details need a verified basis in your business. A tool may repeat details it was given or invent plausible ones, so check which happened.

Proofread Names and Numbers Last and Slowly

Proofreading for typos is ordinary. Proofreading an AI-assisted draft requires a second, separate pass aimed only at proper nouns and numbers, because language models can silently alter them. A tool may swap a street name for a more common one, round a figure, or adjust a spelling toward whatever appeared more often in its training, and the surrounding sentence will read perfectly.

Do this pass slowly, out loud if you can. Reading aloud forces your eyes onto each word instead of letting your brain autocomplete the line.

A hypothetical real estate photographer catches three errors this way in one article. The draft renamed a local neighborhood to a similar-sounding one from another state. It changed her turnaround commitment from the one she actually makes to a rounder number. And it rendered her business name with an ampersand when she spells out "and" everywhere, a detail nobody else would notice and she would never miss.

Check every person's name against how they actually spell it, and if you name any client or partner, confirm you have their explicit permission first. No permission, no name, no exceptions, and never invent a customer or a testimonial to fill the gap. Check every number against your facts inventory, not against your memory. Check your own phone number by dialing it off the page. Check your address, your town names, your service names, your hours. These details are where a reader's trust is either confirmed or quietly lost, because the reader who spots a wrong street name starts wondering what else is wrong.

Review the Excerpt and the Share Copy

The article is not the only thing you are publishing. The excerpt, the meta description, and the social share copy are small, and they are the first words most people will ever see from this piece. They deserve the same judgment as the article, and they usually get none.

Write the excerpt yourself or edit the generated one hard. One or two sentences, stating plainly what the reader will learn, in your voice. A hypothetical fitness studio owner publishing a piece on training after fifty gets a tool-generated excerpt promising to "unlock the secrets of lifelong vitality." Nothing in her article unlocks a secret. She rewrites it to say what the piece actually covers, how she modifies three common exercises for older adults and why. The revised version tells the reader what the article covers. It does not promise an outcome the article cannot support.

Hold share copy to the claim standard too. If the excerpt promises something, the article must deliver it, and the excerpt must not smuggle in a claim that died in your audit. A results promise that got cut from the body does not get to sneak back in through the meta description.

Then preview how the piece renders when shared, the image, the headline, the description as a unit. The share card is a tiny ad for the article, written in a careless moment. Give that preview a deliberate review before release.

Verify the Published Page in a Real Browser

Publishing is not the finish line. Verification is. The gap between what you submitted to your website platform and what a visitor actually sees is real, and it is where good work quietly breaks.

After the piece goes live, open the actual URL in your own browser. Not the editor preview. The live page, the way a stranger would reach it. Read it top to bottom one more time. Formatting shifts in the trip from draft to published page. Headings lose their size, spacing collapses, a stray fragment of markup shows up as visible text.

Click every link on the page yourself and watch where each one lands. Then check the page on your phone, because that is how a large share of your readers will meet it, and a layout that looks clean on a desktop can be a mess on a small screen.

A hypothetical accountant publishes her quarterly tax reminder piece, checks the live page, and finds her platform stripped every internal link during import, leaving plain text where navigation should be. Fifteen minutes of checking catches what would otherwise sit broken for months.

This verification habit scales beyond publishing. Any time a tool acts on your behalf, posting, updating, sending, the confirmation message is not proof. Checking the actual result provides evidence of what happened at that surface. It does not prove that a reader saw the post or took the next step. I wrote more about that boundary in a piece on giving AI access while keeping human control, and the principle is identical here. The machine does the task. You confirm the outcome.

The Checklist, and One Next Step

Everything above compresses into a checklist you can run on every article. Print it, pin it, use it until it is muscle memory.

  • Written down a real reader question, in the reader's own words
  • Gathered source material you own or have personally verified
  • Separated claims from voice before drafting
  • Voice brief on one page: patterns, real phrases, banned words
  • Facts inventory complete, pulled from authoritative sources, not memory
  • Outline built around the reader's experience, with bullets per section
  • Drafted section by section, cutting anything not sourced from your inventory
  • Every claim audited against a source you can defend on the phone
  • Internal links placed only where they help, each one clicked and confirmed
  • Repetition cut, generic filler replaced with your specifics
  • Names and numbers proofread slowly, aloud, against the inventory
  • Excerpt and share copy rewritten to match what the article delivers
  • Live page verified in a real browser, desktop and phone, every link tested

The effort varies with the subject and the source material. Record drafting and review time together when you judge whether the workflow helps. A quick draft that requires extensive correction may not save time.

AI writing tools are worth using. They can help with a first draft, an outline or a difficult paragraph. But the voice is yours, the facts are yours, and the judgment about what a reader deserves is yours, and no tool takes those jobs off your plate. The finished article still needs to earn the reader’s attention through useful, checked information.

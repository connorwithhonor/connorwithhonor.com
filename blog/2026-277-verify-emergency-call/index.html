Before You Send Money, Verify the Emergency Call

Connor MacIvor · October 4, 2026 · From the AI with Honor LIVE

An urgent voice is a reason to verify the request. Call back using a number you already know.

Watch this excerpt from the live conversation

The scenario from the live show

During my October 4 live conversation, I used a hypothetical example: someone calls claiming to be stranded in Mississippi and asks for money. Then you call the actual person and discover they are asleep. The story illustrates a useful habit. Verify who is asking before acting on an urgent request. It was an example, not an account of a documented victim.

I also discussed family safe phrases during the broadcast. This companion guide makes an important limit explicit: a private phrase can be an additional check, but it is not a guarantee of identity or safety. The practical goal is to use independent information and established contacts. You do not need to believe every prediction about AI to prepare a calm response to a suspicious request.

A familiar voice creates an emotional connection, especially when the speaker sounds frightened. Give yourself a response you can follow before that emotion arrives. If there is an immediate threat or someone may be in immediate danger, contact the appropriate emergency services using an independently obtained official contact. This article is general preparation, not an emergency response service.

A familiar voice does not establish identity

Hearing a familiar voice can make you feel that you already know who is calling. AI can generate speech that resembles a real person from recorded audio. The result does not need to fool everyone to create a problem for one worried relative. Recognizing the sound is a starting impression. It does not establish that the person asking for money is actually your sibling, parent or child.

Sound and identity are different things. A recording can capture the voice of a real person without giving a later caller that person’s authority. Public videos and other recordings can also provide material for imitation. You do not need to decide which technology produced the incoming voice before checking the request. Your next step is to reach the person through a contact route you already trust.

Give yourself a separate way to check. When someone claims an emergency, wanting to help is understandable. Keep that concern while stepping outside the incoming caller’s instructions. A number they dictate during the call comes from the same unverified source as the story. An established contact, or another trusted person you reach independently, gives you a different place to ask what is happening.

End the call and use a number you already know

The most direct method of verification is to end the current call and dial a phone number that is already known to be associated with the alleged relative. This number should come from a saved contact, an existing trusted record, or an independently verified official contact, not from any information provided by the incoming caller. By initiating the connection through a trusted channel, the recipient can speak directly with the person they intend to reach.

In the hypothetical scenario, after hearing an urgent breakdown story, the listener might pause and say they will call back. The call is then ended, and the known number for that family member is dialed. Ask the person you reach whether they made the request and what is happening. A familiar greeting alone does not answer that question. A call going unanswered does not prove the original story true or false.

The callback gives you another source of information outside the incoming caller’s instructions. Ask about the actual request, not just whether the person sounds familiar. Describe what was requested and where the money was supposed to go. The callback is one verification step. It is not a guarantee that every account, device or detail involved in a situation is secure.

If the person does not answer

There are circumstances where a known relative cannot be reached by phone. Cell service may be limited, the battery may have died, or the individual may be in an area without coverage. In these cases, the inability to connect does not automatically confirm or deny the emergency story, but it does influence next steps.

When a call goes unanswered after attempting to redial the known number, the caller may try alternative methods of contact. This could include reaching out to other family members, friends, or colleagues who might know the relative’s whereabouts. The goal is to gather information from independent sources that can either corroborate or challenge the story received over the phone.

If multiple attempts to reach the person fail, and no other contacts are available, the situation remains uncertain. At this point, the focus shifts to recognizing other red flags within the call itself, such as demands for specific types of payment or unusual urgency, that may indicate a scam regardless of whether the relative is reached.

A private phrase is an additional check

Some families establish private phrases or questions known only to family members. These can serve as an additional verification step when used alongside other methods. For example, a phrase chosen years ago might be asked during a suspicious call to see if the caller knows it.

However, such phrases have limitations. They can be discovered through social engineering, forgotten over time, or inadvertently shared with others. A scammer who has gathered personal information about a family might guess or obtain the phrase through deception. Additionally, if the phrase is written down or stored digitally, it may be accessible to someone with malicious intent.

Therefore, a private family code word should be viewed as one tool among many, not as definitive proof of identity. Its value lies in supplementing other verification efforts, such as calling back on a known number, or prompting additional questions that only the genuine relative would know. The phrase alone should never be the sole basis for releasing money or information.

Notice urgency, secrecy and payment demands

Scammers often create a sense of urgency to short-circuit normal verification processes. They may insist that immediate action is required, that the situation will worsen if help is delayed, or that discretion is essential to protect the relative’s reputation or legal standing. This pressure tactic aims to prevent the recipient from taking time to think or consult others.

Similarly, demands for payment methods that are difficult to reverse or trace, such as wire transfers, prepaid debit cards, or cryptocurrency, are common indicators of fraud. A demand for one of these methods deserves a separate check, especially when the caller tries to prevent you from contacting anyone else.

Recognizing these patterns does not mean every urgent call is a scam, but it does suggest a need for heightened caution. Treat that combination as a reason to stop and check before sending anything. You do not need to calculate a probability or diagnose a particular scam to refuse an unverified transfer.

Write down the process, not the private phrase

Developing a family response plan can provide structure for handling suspicious calls. This plan might include steps such as: ending the call, attempting to reach the relative on a known number, contacting another trusted person, and discussing the incident afterward. The plan should be agreed upon when everyone is calm, not during a moment of crisis.

An important aspect of such a plan is deciding how to verify identity without compromising security. For families that use a private phrase, the agreement might focus on how and when to use it, rather than prescribing it as a guarantee. The plan could also include designating specific trusted contacts who can be called if the primary relative is unreachable.

Keep the written plan somewhere the intended people can use it. Protect contact details and agreed checks from public sharing. A family plan that contains everyone’s private information should not become a public example on social media. Review the contact routes when circumstances change, and explain the update directly to the people who need it. A forgotten document cannot do that work by itself.

If money or information was already shared

If money has been sent or personal information disclosed in response to a suspicious call, immediate action is recommended. Contacting the payment provider or financial institution to report the transaction and inquire about reversal options is a necessary first step. While recovery is not guaranteed, the provider can explain what action is available for that particular transaction. Keep a record of the report and any instructions you receive.

If personal information has been shared, such as account numbers, passwords, or social security details, monitoring associated accounts for unusual activity becomes important. Changing passwords, enabling additional authentication where available, and alerting relevant institutions can help mitigate further risk.

Reporting the incident to appropriate authorities, such as the Federal Trade Commission through their designated channels, contributes to broader efforts to track and stop scams. Even if no financial loss occurred, sharing the experience helps build a record that may prevent others from falling victim to similar tactics.

Practice when nobody is in trouble

Practicing verification steps when no emergency is present can make them feel more natural during an actual stressful situation. Families might role-play a scenario where one member receives a concerning call, and together they walk through the process of ending the call, dialing a known number, and checking in with another relative. This rehearsal builds familiarity with the steps and reduces the likelihood of skipping critical verification in a moment of panic.

The point I want you to take away is that voice recognition, while familiar, is not sufficient on its own to confirm identity in the age of advanced audio synthesis. A combination of independent callbacks, contact with other trusted individuals, awareness of urgency and payment tactics, and established family protocols creates a stronger defense against deception. No single method is foolproof. These steps give you a practical process for checking a request before responding.

In moments where immediate danger is perceived, such as threats of harm or ongoing emergencies, contacting official emergency services through independently obtained numbers remains the appropriate course of action. This guidance is general in nature and not a substitute for professional advice or legal counsel. By staying informed and prepared, individuals can better protect themselves and their loved ones from evolving forms of deception.

Give the household an easy first sentence

A complicated response plan may be difficult to remember under pressure. Agree on a simple opening that expresses care without accepting the caller’s instructions. For example: I want to help, and I am going to verify this through our usual contact. That is proposed wording for a household exercise, not a quotation from a real emergency or a promise that every caller will respond calmly.

Practice saying it aloud with the people who will use the plan. Notice whether the wording feels natural. An older relative may prefer a short sentence written near the phone. Someone who uses a mobile phone may want the trusted contacts easy to find. The useful question is whether the intended person can follow the process, not whether the document sounds sophisticated.

In a fictional rehearsal, the caller insists that hanging up will make the situation worse. The person practicing should recognize that pressure and follow the agreed verification route. The exercise can stop immediately if it becomes upsetting. There is no need to imitate violence, impersonate a real institution, or frighten a family member to make the point.

Do not confuse a practice script with universal instructions for an actual emergency. If someone may be in immediate danger, involve appropriate emergency services. If the situation is uncertain, explain that uncertainty when seeking help. The household plan supports communication and verification; it does not replace the judgment or resources of qualified responders.

Make the contact list usable before it matters

A useful contact list does not need to contain a family’s entire private history. Start with the people who have agreed to help verify a concerning request and the ordinary ways to reach them. Confirm the entries directly during a calm conversation. A number copied from an old message may no longer reach the intended person, and an outdated contact can add confusion.

For a hypothetical household, a parent might have a child’s established number and another trusted relative’s contact. The child might keep a similar route for reaching the parent. Discuss who is comfortable being contacted and under what circumstances. Avoid assuming that someone is available simply because their name appears in a phone. The agreement matters as much as the entry.

Consider accessibility. Can the person read the contact names, hear a call clearly, or use the device comfortably? Would a second trusted person help with verification when communication is difficult? Make the plan usable for the people involved without sharing their private information broadly. Do not publish contact lists or private phrases as an example for others.

Review the list when a number, household arrangement or trusted contact changes. This does not require a complicated schedule. It requires someone to take responsibility for keeping the chosen route useful. A simple current record can support a calmer response than a detailed document that nobody has looked at since it was created.

Walk through a fictional stranded-relative call

Imagine a fictional family member receives a call from someone claiming to be stranded after a vehicle problem. The voice sounds familiar. The caller asks for an immediate transfer to a person the family member has never met. The recipient feels a strong urge to fix the problem quickly. That emotional response is understandable; the exercise is about what to do with it.

The recipient ends the incoming call and uses an existing trusted contact to reach the relative directly. If the relative answers, the recipient asks whether they made the request and describes the situation clearly. If the relative says they did not call and are not stranded, the original demand should not be treated as a verified request from that relative.

If the call goes unanswered, the exercise remains unresolved. The family member can contact another agreed trusted person and explain what was received. They should not fill the gap with an assumption that the silence confirms danger, and they should not assume that everything is fine merely because the story sounds unusual. If there is an immediate safety concern, contact the appropriate official service.

After the rehearsal, discuss the confusing moments. Was the trusted number easy to find? Did the person practicing know whom to contact next? Did they feel pressured to stay on the incoming call? Those observations help improve the plan. They are more useful than declaring that somebody passed or failed a test of intelligence.

Keep a private phrase in its proper role

A household that chooses a private phrase should discuss how it will be used and what happens if someone cannot remember it. Forgetting a phrase during stress does not prove that a caller is an impostor. Knowing a phrase does not prove that every part of a request is legitimate. Treat either outcome as information to consider alongside independent verification.

The response plan can describe the process without containing the phrase itself. For example, it can say to use the agreed check and then verify the request through the established contact route. This keeps a document shared for preparation from becoming a document that exposes the private check. Decide who needs to know the phrase rather than circulating it casually.

Choose a phrase that is practical for the people who must remember and say it. A clever sequence that nobody can recall may be less useful than expected. Discuss language, hearing and memory needs without shaming anyone. The purpose is to help people communicate under pressure, not to create another source of embarrassment or confusion.

If the phrase is exposed, discuss replacing it through an appropriate private conversation. Do not describe exposure as proof that every account is compromised or that a specific device must have recorded it. Investigate the actual circumstances. Keep the remedy proportionate to what is known, and continue to rely on more than a phrase before releasing money or sensitive information.

Support the person who received the call

An upsetting call can leave someone embarrassed even when they did not send anything. Make it easy for them to tell a trusted person what happened. A response that starts with blame can make future reporting harder. The useful information is what the caller asked for, what was shared, what action occurred and what still needs attention.

Consider a fictional relative who says they almost transferred money but stopped. Ask what helped them pause and what made the request convincing. Maybe the voice was familiar, the timing was plausible, or the caller mentioned something recognizable. Use that account to improve the response plan. Do not turn it into a public anecdote without the person’s permission.

If money was sent, focus on the next concrete action with the relevant provider and appropriate reporting channel. Avoid promising recovery or recommending an unfamiliar person who claims they can retrieve the funds. Keep receipts and reference numbers in an appropriate private place. A clear record can help the affected person explain the situation without having to reconstruct it repeatedly.

The same supportive approach applies at work. Employees should know whom to contact about an unexpected payment or account request. A business process that rewards early questions can be easier to operate than one where people feel they must hide uncertainty. The exact controls will depend on the business, but the responsibility to respond should be clear.

Review the request, not just the voice

Even a genuine caller can relay incorrect information or misunderstand what is needed. Once identity has been checked, the requested action still deserves attention. Who is supposed to receive the money? What is the stated purpose? Is there an independently verified way to contact the organization involved? Do not let one successful check answer every other question automatically.

For a fictional business example, a familiar coworker might forward an invoice without realizing that payment details have changed. Recognizing the coworker’s voice would not establish that the invoice is correct. The appropriate person should use the business’s established verification process for payment changes. That is a different task from deciding whether a caller sounds like a known colleague.

AI adds another reason to be careful about appearances, but the working habit is broader. Separate the claimed identity, the facts of the situation, the proposed action and the evidence supporting it. This is the same general habit discussed in A Polished AI Checklist Can Still Miss the Task That Matters: compare a convincing presentation with the actual requirements before acting.

A useful next step

Have a calm conversation with the people who would call you in a difficult situation. Agree on an independent contact route, identify another trusted person, and practice finding the right contact. Keep private phrases and personal information out of public examples. If a real emergency occurs, use the appropriate emergency services rather than treating an online article or a chatbot as the response team.

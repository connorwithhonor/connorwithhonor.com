Leading Your Office Into AI: What Brokers Do First

November 8, 2026 // AI With Honor // Connor MacIvor

Practice run, recorded Nov 8, 2026. Not the live talk.

TL;DRStart with a list, not a tool. Sort every task into automate, assist, or keep human. Hand AI access in steps, like a Platinum card. Humans dial: an AI voice calling cell phones without written consent can cost up to $1,500 a call. And your next sellers are already in your database.

The broom that learns

You probably remember the Sorcerer's Apprentice. The apprentice gets tired of carrying water, borrows his boss's spell book, brings a broom to life and tells it to carry the water. Then he takes a nap. The broom does exactly what it was told. Bucket after bucket. The house floods.

Last month in Prague, at the Forum 2000 conference, the historian Yuval Noah Harari called that cartoon the best way to understand artificial intelligence. Then, in my words, he said the cartoon gets one thing wrong. The cartoon broom only does what it was told. The broom we are building learns. It finds new ways to carry the water. It starts doing things nobody asked for.

For a broker, your office is the house. Your agents, your clients, your files, your license. The broom is already inside. The question is who is watching it.

AI is not a tech project. It is a margin project.

Your margin as a broker comes from three places:

  1. Agents who produce more without costing you more.
  2. Files that never put your license at risk.
  3. Business that comes from relationships you already paid for.

Every part of the talk answers one of three questions. What do we automate so agents get hours back? What do we protect so nobody in the office ends up explaining to a regulator what the software did? And how do we find tomorrow's sellers?

1998 versus today

I got my license in 1998. Pager on the belt. A fax machine that jammed every other Tuesday. An MLS book thick enough to prop a door open. Index cards in a shoebox.

The agents who won back then called the most people and remembered every one of them. The birthday. The kid leaving for college. That is still the winning move. What changed is the price. Remembering used to cost an agent their evenings and weekends. Today the phone in their pocket can read a 40-page disclosure packet faster than you can pour coffee, draft a follow-up in their own voice, and keep track of every conversation. When the cost of remembering drops close to zero, the people who move first eat everybody else's lunch.

Start with a list, not a tool

Every broker asks the same first question: which AI should we buy? Wrong question. The first move costs nothing, and it is not a tool. It is a list.

Before anybody in your office buys anything, every agent writes down what they actually did all week, Monday through Sunday, with the hours next to each task. Not what they think they do. What they did.

You will be amazed. The agent who swears they prospect all day finds out they spent a full working day formatting flyers, chasing a lender for one document, and retyping the same email to six different buyers. That list is your AI plan. Nothing gets automated that is not on it.

Here is the part that surprises every room. A Realtors Property Resource survey this year found that 82% of agents already use AI in their business. In NAR's 2025 technology survey, only 17% said AI made a significant positive impact. Lots of people using it. Few getting paid for it. The difference is the list.

The worksheet is on the broker page. If you do one thing from this article, do that one.

Three buckets

Once the list exists, every task goes into one of three buckets.

  • Automate. The AI does it and a human checks it before it goes anywhere. Follow-up drafts, market summaries, first drafts of listing copy, meeting notes, checklists.
  • Assist. The AI helps, but your agent drives. Pricing prep, practicing objections before a listing appointment, research before a first meeting.
  • Keep human. No machine, ever. Negotiation. Fiduciary advice. Anything that gets signed. Any conversation with a client about money. Any judgment call that touches fair housing.

That third bucket belongs to you personally, because that is where the liability lives.

For managers: collect every agent's list and lay them side by side. You will see the same handful of tasks on almost every sheet. Build one office template for each and train one per sales meeting. That is your next quarter of training, already written, and your agents will use it because it came from their own lists.

Pick by the task, not the logo

Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot are converging. They all write, read documents, search the web, remember instructions and work in projects. Every few months one jumps ahead and another catches up. If you write your office policy around a logo, you will rewrite it by spring.

Pick by the task. Take one real disclosure packet, have three agents run it through three tools, and keep the one your people like using. If your office already lives in Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, look there first, because your files are already sitting in it.

One rule does not bend. Client information only goes into a business or team plan where training on your data is turned off and an administrator controls the accounts. The free app on an agent's phone is for public information.

The Platinum Card rule

You would never hand a brand-new agent your American Express Platinum card on day one. Not because they are a bad person. Because you have not watched them yet. You give a little access, you see what they do with it, and they earn more.

AI gets the same treatment, and it matters more every month, because the newest tools do not just write. They connect to email, the calendar, the CRM and your files. A tool that can read your inbox can also send from it.

Hand over access in steps:

  1. Read only. It reads and summarizes. It changes nothing.
  2. Draft only. It writes. A human hits send, every time.
  3. Act with approval. It proposes, and waits for a human to click yes.
  4. Act within limits. Low-risk sends only, by written exception from the broker.

A task moves up one step after a clean month, with your sign-off. It drops a step the day it makes a mistake. And some things never happen at any step: no signing, no offers or contracts going out, no legal or tax advice, no money talk with a client.

The $1,500 phone call

There are products being sold to agents right now that will call your entire database with an AI voice that sounds exactly like a person. Fast. Cheap. A trap.

In February 2024, the FCC ruled that AI-generated voices count as artificial voices under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act. A marketing call to a cell phone using an AI voice needs prior express written consent, and an open house sign-in sheet does not get you there. Without consent, damages run $500 per call, and up to $1,500 per call if the violation was willful.

Do the math. One agent, one afternoon, 300 calls at $1,500 each is $450,000. These cases get filed as class actions.

So the rule in your office is simple. Humans dial. Scrub the Do Not Call list. Anyone who wants to automate calling goes through you and your counsel first.

Tomorrow's sellers are already in your database

Picture a solid agent in your office with 2,000 names between their phone and their CRM. Past clients, open house sign-ins, neighbors from the last listing. How many heard from that agent in the last 12 months? In most offices, a few hundred.

The rest are people who already know and like your agent and will sell a house one day to whoever calls first. That is the cheapest lead source in the business, you already paid for it, and it is exactly the job AI does well: clean the list, find who has gone quiet, and draft a personal note your agent reviews and sends.

Score by the property, never by the person. Years owned, property type, past relationship. Never by age, family, or any protected characteristic. And keep your list in a form you own and can export. I wrote more about why owning your list matters in Digital Vassals.

The broker's job now

Harari ended his talk on hope, not cheap hope. He said one of the most dangerous ideas going around is that everybody only cares about money and power. If that were true, nobody could trust anybody. The poison is cynicism. Start with yourself. Most people care about the truth and the people in their lives.

In a world filling up with fake, the most valuable thing your office can be is the place people know is real. The broom is learning, and it is coming fast. But we are still in the room. We still get a vote on what the broom touches. That is the job of a broker now.

The Machine

Have you ever wondered if you are being recorded? Maybe the question has never even crossed your mind. If you are under 30, it probably has not, because you grew up assuming the answer. For the rest of us, that assumption is brand new, and the technology arriving this Christmas is about to make it permanent.

The First Viral Camera Was A Camcorder On A Balcony

My education on this started in 1991, before I ever pinned on an LAPD badge. I was still in the police academy when a man named George Holliday stepped onto his balcony in the Foothill Division area with a camcorder and filmed officers beating Rodney King. The story goes that Holliday first brought the tape to a police station and tried to get an investigation started, and the desk officer waved him off. So he took it to a TV station instead. Everybody knows what happened after that.

That tape was the first glimpse for my generation that privacy was already gone. One man, one camera, one balcony, and the whole world saw what happened on a street in Lake View Terrace. It only took one lens back then. Keep that math in your head, because we are about to multiply it by everybody.

You Already Signed The Permission Slip

Here is the part most people skip: you agreed to this. Somewhere between your smart TV setup screen, your last iPhone purchase, and the software update you accepted without reading, you signed a terms of service agreement. Buried in that wall of text was language where you gave away a couple of rights. Maybe a lot of rights. Nobody reads it. Everybody clicks it. The microphones in your living room did not sneak in, they were invited, and the invitation had your signature on it.

Is AI Making Us Dumber Or Just Doing The Remembering?

The more AI listens, the more it can remember for you, and there is a real argument about whether that makes us sharper or lazier. I lean lazier. Look at what already happened. Nobody memorizes routes anymore, we follow the mapping app. Around Los Angeles that is survival, because when the app says take Balboa instead of the 405, you take Balboa and save yourself 40 or 45 minutes depending on commute flow.

Same with phone numbers. I still have the number I grew up with memorized. It is not in service anymore, and there is nobody left to answer it, everybody on that end is gone. But it is burned in. Now? Most of us have maybe two numbers memorized and not many email addresses either, because they just pop up when we type a name. The tool carries the memory, so the muscle let go. Recording is next in that chain, and it is a much bigger link.

Assume The Room Is Recording

If you are giving a talk or sitting in an audience, somebody is capturing it. That is settled. The speaker is recording, the event is recording, and half the crowd has a phone out. In those venues there is a kind of shared understanding, you perform accordingly.

The one-on-one meeting is where the ground is shifting under your feet. Two people, a coffee shop, a conference room, a truck cab. You might go off the cuff. You might say something spicy that plays very differently as a 12-second clip than it did in the flow of a real conversation. And you have no idea whether the person across from you is wearing a device that captures all of it.

The One Question To Ask Before Every Meeting

Ask it straight, before the meeting starts: I am not going to record you, and I do not want you to record me in any way. Is that clear? I think that should become common practice, the same way we confirm a meeting time. Both people establish it out loud, both people acknowledge it.

I am not an attorney and this is not legal advice. But once you have both said it, you have made a record of the agreement itself. If a recording surfaces later anyway, the conversation about that recording starts from very different footing. Unless you are talking about hurting people or doing something truly horrible, the agreement should hold, and if somebody burns it, they burned it after looking you in the eye and agreeing not to.

The camera stopped being an event in 1991. Now it is becoming the default setting on the human body.

"Off The Record" Is Not A Thing Anymore

Say something spicy enough and it will get airtime no matter what was agreed. That is just the truth of how content works now. A voice clip of a stranger melting down in a supermarket line is fodder, and fodder gets posted. It is the spoken-word version of what I covered when I asked whether you should send your business idea to an influencer whose AI is reading it: once your words leave your mouth near a capture device, you no longer control where they land. Which is exactly why the ask-first habit matters. It will not make you bulletproof. It makes you deliberate, and deliberate people say fewer things that end up as somebody else's monetized clip.

The Wearable Wave Arriving This Christmas

The end of 2026 is shaping up to be the season of the always-on recorder. Little buttons. Little cards. Pendants like the Limitless Pendant, the Bee bracelet that Amazon just scooped up, the Friend pendant that listens so it can form thoughts about your day. Watch apps that used to require a deliberate tap are being built in around the idea of simply recording all day long.

Here is the mechanism that changes everything. The device is not the product, the pipeline is. Your wearable catches a signal near your home computer or Wi-Fi, dumps the day into your AI, and the system structures it: dates it, organizes it, indexes every conversation you had in your travels. The barista at Starbucks. The voice at the Sonic drive-thru when you grabbed that great hamburger. And the people closest to you, at your own kitchen table.

A large language model can extrapolate an entire day of spoken words across multiple people and multiple places without breaking a sweat. That is not a camcorder on a balcony. That is total recall, worn as jewelry, and it is going to become so routine that we will not even remember we are recording until something surfaces later. The same way a crowd now pulls out phones at a car crash instead of running to help, the next generation will record everybody by reflex, because everybody will be recording them right back.

When Everyone Records, Baiting Becomes A Business

Once the capture is constant, provoking a reaction becomes a content strategy. You have already seen the auditor channels, the guys who plant a camera outside a waxing studio or a high-end jewelry store and wait for the confrontation. I do not fully understand the method, but the views are real and the channel is probably monetized. Security gets called, sometimes law enforcement shows up just to clear a sidewalk, sometimes somebody gets hooked and hauled off, and sometimes an attorney is standing by to turn the whole encounter into a payday.

Now shrink that camera to a shirt button and hand it to everybody. Somebody cuts in front of you at the supermarket on purpose, hoping you pop off, because your worst 15 seconds is their next post. Depending on who you are around and what you say, this stuff can bite you in the keystone. Watch out for yourselves. The world is getting more interesting by the day.

The Door Knockers Are Already Wearing Them

Sales agents going door-to-door are using these devices right now. When an agent connects with a homeowner, the AI knows which house they were standing at, who the homeowner was, and exactly how the conversation went. Whether the answer was thank you for stopping by, I have been thinking about it, or an F-off and a threat to call the cops, all of that confluence gets captured and filed.

And here is my question, the one I keep coming back to: did that agent tell the person at the door they were recording? In California, Penal Code 632 generally requires every party's consent before recording a confidential conversation. I am not an attorney, but I spent 20 years with LAPD and 23 total in law enforcement, and I can smell a legal storm forming. This could become a very real issue very, very soon, and the businesses deploying these tools without a disclosure protocol are the ones standing in the open when it hits.

One-Party Vs All-Party Consent: Which Rules Cover You?

Recording law in the United States splits into two camps, and which camp you are standing in changes everything. The federal baseline and most states are one-party consent: if one person in the conversation agrees to the recording, and that person can be the one holding the recorder, it is generally allowed. A smaller group of states, with California, Florida, Washington, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, and Illinois among them, require every party in a confidential conversation to consent before recording is lawful.

Notice what that means in practice. The guy visiting from a one-party state, wearing a pendant that has been perfectly fine at home, walks into a coffee meeting in Santa Clarita and is now operating under California rules. The conversation happens where the strictest law applies, not where the device was purchased. Cross-state phone calls get even messier, because both ends of the line can sit under different rules at the same time.

And the wearable wave scrambles all of it. These consent frameworks were written for a world where recording was a decision, somebody pressed a button. An always-on pendant never presses a button. It just runs. Whether a device that records everything by default can ever satisfy an all-party consent standard in a private conversation is exactly the kind of question courts have not fully answered yet. I am not an attorney, and I would not want to be the test case either.

The Recording Protocol: Five Rules To Run Starting Today

You cannot opt out of the recorded world, but you can walk through it deliberately. Five rules.

Rule 1: Assume capture, everywhere. The supermarket line, the drive-thru, the sidewalk, the open house, the sales floor. If a human being is present, treat a microphone as present. This is not paranoia, it is the same posture I carried for 23 years in law enforcement: conduct yourself like the tape exists, because one day it will.

Rule 2: Ask the question before every one-on-one. The two sentences from earlier in this post. I am not going to record you, I do not want you to record me, is that clear? Thirty seconds of mild awkwardness in exchange for a spoken agreement both people heard.

Rule 3: Never say off the record what you cannot survive on the record. The agreement in Rule 2 is a deterrent, not a force field. If the words would end a deal, a friendship, or a career as a 12-second clip, do not put them in the air.

Rule 4: Put it in writing for business meetings. A single line in the calendar invite, this meeting is not to be recorded by either party, costs nothing and stamps the agreement with a timestamp before anybody sits down. When my team runs client sessions, that line rides in the invite by default.

Rule 5: Audit your own pockets. Your phone, your watch, your car, and any AI note-taker you installed six months ago and forgot about. Know what is capturing, where the audio goes, and how long it lives. You cannot ask somebody else the recording question with a clear conscience while your own wrist is filing the conversation.

What A Business Owner Should Do About This Now

If your business records anything, calls, door approaches, front-desk chatter, meetings, and feeds any of it into AI, you need rules before the law writes them for you. Disclosure language. Consent gates. A straight answer to the question, where does this audio go and who can pull it up later. When I build AI voice agents for businesses through HireAIVoice, the California disclosure gates are built into the script before the agent ever takes a call, because bolting compliance on after the lawsuit is the expensive order of operations.

The same discipline applies to every AI tool in your shop. It is the reason I tell every operator to push back on AI output before publishing it, to set up their AI accounts with the privacy toggles actually understood, and to know why AI makes things up in the first place. The tools are phenomenal. Unsupervised, they are phenomenal liabilities.

Common Questions

Is it legal to record a conversation without permission in California?

California Penal Code 632 generally requires the consent of every party before recording a confidential communication, meaning a conversation where someone reasonably expects privacy. Public settings with no reasonable expectation of privacy are treated differently. I am not an attorney and this is not legal advice, so talk to one before you rely on any of it. The practical rule is simpler: assume you are being recorded, and say out loud what you both agree to before the conversation starts.

Are AI wearables really recording all the time?

The always-on category is designed to do exactly that. Devices like the Limitless Pendant, the Bee bracelet that Amazon acquired, and the Friend pendant capture ambient audio through the day, transcribe it, and feed it to an AI that structures, dates, and organizes every conversation. The holiday season at the end of 2026 is expected to push these devices, plus built-in watch and phone apps that do the same job, into the mainstream.

How do I know if someone is recording me in a meeting?

Most of the time you cannot know. A pendant looks like jewelry, a card sits in a shirt pocket, and a watch app shows nothing at all. That is why the answer is not detection, it is agreement: ask directly at the start of the meeting, state that you are not recording, and ask the other person to confirm the same thing in both directions.

What should I say before a one-on-one meeting?

Keep it to two sentences: I want us both on the same page, I am not going to record you and I do not want you to record me in any way. Is that clear? Once both people acknowledge it out loud, you have made a record of the agreement itself, which matters if a recording surfaces later. I am not an attorney, but making the record beats staying silent every time.

They Don't Want To Help You. They Want Your Idea.

Let me set the scene, because you have watched this exact video. Somebody is talking to the camera and they are good at it. They tell you they are building a channel where they actually listen. Send me your workflow. Send me your strategy. Tell me how you are using artificial intelligence in your business and let us make it better together. And it will not cost you a dime, because I just want to help.

You believe it. Why would you not? They are looking dead into the lens and they are not reading a script at that moment. They mean it in the room. And there is a jolt that runs through you when you think that Connor is going to look at this, or Peter is going to look at this, or Alex is going to look at this. Fill in whichever name gets your heart rate up.

So you write it out. The thing you have been circling for two years. The problem nobody solved because nobody else got close enough to it to see it. You hit send.

What Actually Happens When You Hit Send

At any real scale, a person is not reading it first. An automation is. Your email lands in a workflow that parses the text, sorts it, ranks it against whatever the operator told it to look for, and surfaces the small percentage that scored well to an actual human being. Everyone else gets a warm, personal-sounding response so the loop stays open and the submissions keep coming.

I want to be fair here, because this is not automatically villainy. If you get four thousand emails a week, you cannot read four thousand emails a week. Something has to triage. Some of these people are running that system honestly and genuinely surfacing the good stuff to help. I have built systems like this. That is the point. I know exactly what they can do, which is why I can tell you what they are doing.

And what they can do now is the part that changed. Reading a mountain of messy, unstructured human writing and pulling the patterns out of it is close to the single thing these models are best at on earth. Your submission shows up pre-sorted by intent, described in detail by the one person who actually lives the problem, and it costs the recipient nothing. There has never been a cheaper source of validated market research in the history of business. I walked through what happens when you let a machine run that loop end to end in A Machine Built A Whole Company.

The price of admission to your ideas got set at free. You are the one who set it.

Then comes the part that stings. Something in your email sparks. It solves a problem the operator did not even know existed, because they are not a nurse, not a trustee, not a stockbroker, not the person running the front desk at a medical office at seven in the morning. You are. You saw the hole because you have been standing in it. That is the whole reason the idea is good.

They read it and think, huh, I did not realize that was broken. And they have the audience, the team, the capital, and the build speed you were still assembling. So they build it. They own it. They sell it to everybody, including you. And you handed it over out of nothing but goodwill, because somebody you admired asked nicely.

Why The Standard Advice Just Broke

Go search this question right now and you will get a wall of unanimous advice telling you the opposite of everything I just said. Share your idea with anyone who will listen. Ideas are a dime a dozen. Execution is the only thing that matters. Nobody is going to steal it, because companies do not chase unproven concepts, they wait until you have proven the market and then compete.

Here is the uncomfortable part. That advice was correct. For twenty years it was some of the best advice in business, and I gave it myself. It was correct because acting on a stranger's idea was expensive. It meant hiring people, writing specifications, burning six months and real money on something unvalidated. Almost nobody would do that on a whim, so talking openly cost you nothing and bought you feedback, partners, and customers.

What broke is not human nature. What broke is the price of acting. When a competent operator can read ten thousand submissions in an afternoon, pick the three with real signal, and stand up a working version in a matter of days, the arithmetic that made open sharing safe does not compute the same way anymore. The advice did not become wrong because people got worse. It became shakier because the build got cheap.

So I am not telling you ideas suddenly beat execution. Execution still wins. I am telling you the gap between having an idea and executing on it used to be your moat, and that moat is a lot narrower than it was when the advice was written.

The Same Commercial, New Decade

None of this is a new play. It is the eighties and nineties infomercial with better production. The Lamborghini in the driveway. The Maserati. The mansion, the beautiful people arranged around the pool wearing as little as legally allowed, and the guy in the middle explaining that you can have all of this too. It is only going to run you two hundred and ninety nine dollars a month.

The modern version dropped the price to zero, and that should get your attention rather than relax you. The course is not the product anymore. You are not being asked for two hundred and ninety nine dollars a month. You are being asked for something they cannot generate on their own, which is the raw, lived, specific knowledge of a few thousand working people who each know exactly where their own industry is bleeding. Free is not the absence of a price. Free means the price moved.

Same energy as the giants flipping a terms-of-service clause and helping themselves to a decade of your photos without so much as a phone call, which I got into in The Invitation Nobody Sent. And the same instinct I keep pushing on this show applies here. When something is handed to you for nothing, find out who gets paid. I ran that same question at the AI job panic in No One Is Coming To Save You From AI, and it holds up every time.

I watched a version of this growing up. A massive industry rolled into my hometown and flattened every mom-and-pop on the street inside a couple of years. And honestly, that is capitalism, and I am not crying about it. They generated real economy. They employed people. But I saw who absorbed the hit and who wrote the press release, and I have never been able to unsee the shape of it.

Why Nobody Close To You Believes It

Here is the second trap, and it is the one that pushes people into a stranger's inbox in the first place. You cannot get a straight read from the people around you, and it is not because they are cruel.

Tell somebody at arm's length and they shrug. It is fine. It is nonsense. Whatever you say. Tell the people who actually love you and it gets worse, because they have the receipts. They remember all fifteen hundred attempts. They remember the drinking and the quitting, the smoking and the quitting, and every project that got announced with fireworks and quietly died. That is not malice. That is memory, and memory is doing its job.

It is the same reflex as announcing a diet. I have lived in that genre, so I am not quoting a book at you here. You tell the room that this is it, the joints hurt, the doctor said the thing, and Monday you are starting. And what you actually see cross their faces is here we go, now everybody gets to suffer through another one of these. I go a lot deeper on that whole announce-it-and-kill-it pattern over at The Last Addiction.

They did not have your scar tissue. They had your idea and a faster build.

So you go looking for a neutral judge with credentials. Somebody who does not remember your last five failures. Somebody whose opinion would settle it. And that is exactly the emotional door the open invitation is built against. It is not aimed at your greed. It is aimed at the part of you that wants one person with standing to say this is good.

Wash Your Face And Say Nothing

Scripture handles this better than any business book I have read. It talks about fasting, and it does not say announce it. It says do not go out there with the long face performing your discipline so everyone knows what you are enduring. Wash your face. Keep your mouth shut. Do the thing in private and let it work.

I think ideas live in that same category. Not because they are magic, but because saying it out loud gives you a little hit of the reward before you have done any of the work, and that hit takes the edge off. The announcement feels like progress. It is the cheapest possible version of the feeling you were supposed to earn by building.

I will tell on myself here. For most of my career I was a completely open book. Everything I was working on, everything I was building, I said out loud to whoever was standing there. And that open book got pages torn out of it. More than once I watched something I had described in detail show up in the market, built by someone with more reach than me, while I was still getting my version ready to ship. Not stolen in any dramatic way. Just executed faster by somebody who heard it early and had a shorter runway to launch.

That is a recent lesson for me, not a lifelong principle. Somebody very close to me, who loves me with no strings on it at all, sat me down and told me to stop doing that. Thank you, Roxy.

What To Do Instead

The move is not paranoia. Do not become Scrooge, do not lock yourself in a room, and do not stop talking to people who can actually help you. The move is sequence. Build before you broadcast.

// The Play
  • Ship the ugly version first. The smallest thing that actually works, in front of one real user. A working prototype is a position. A description in an email is a gift.
  • Share with skin in the game, not with an audience. Partners, advisors, and early customers who carry risk alongside you will give you better feedback than a stranger's inbox, and they have a reason to want you to win.
  • Treat free asks like paid ones. When someone requests your best thinking for nothing, ask what they get out of it. If the honest answer is content, leads, or product direction, you now know the actual price.
  • Keep the receipts. Dates, drafts, versions, a repository with history. It is not a legal strategy. It is knowing your own timeline cold.
  • Do not confuse the announcement with the work. If telling people gives you the same feeling as building, you will keep telling people and never build.

And use the tools. I am not anti-AI and never have been. I use it every single day, I build with it, and I think this is the most access a regular working person has ever been handed. That was the whole point of AI For Everyone, Not Just The Wealthy. This is not a warning about the technology. It is a warning about who is currently pointing it at your inbox and who captures the value when they do.

FAQ

Should I send my business idea to a podcaster or influencer who asks for it?

Usually not, and the reason is structural rather than personal. When you send it, you have no agreement, no timestamp anyone respects, no leverage, and no practical way to show where the idea came from later. The person on the other end has distribution, a team, and the ability to build faster than you can. Ship the smallest working version first, get one real user, then decide who has earned a look.

Do influencers actually read every idea submission they get?

At any meaningful scale, no. Inbound gets routed through an automation that parses, sorts, and ranks it, and a warm auto-reply goes back so the sender feels acknowledged and keeps sending. Human attention lands on the small slice the system flags as interesting. That is not a conspiracy, it is just what running a large inbox looks like now, but you should understand it before you decide what to put in one.

Is AI being used to mine audience submissions for business ideas?

Yes, and it is one of the most natural uses of the technology available today. Taking a high volume of unstructured audience email and pulling patterns, gaps, and repeated pain points out of it is close to a perfect fit for what these models are good at. The submissions arrive already sorted by intent, already described by the person who lives the problem, and already free.

Can someone legally take my idea if I email it to them?

In general, an idea by itself is not the thing that gets protected. Specific expressions, filings, and implementations are where protection lives, which is why an unsolicited email describing a concept is a weak position to negotiate from. This is commentary and general information, not legal advice, so talk to an intellectual property attorney before you rely on any of it. The practical protection is almost always building the thing first.

Why does most startup advice say to share your idea openly?

Because for a long time it was good advice. Execution beat ideas by such a wide margin that talking openly bought you feedback, partners, and customers at almost no real risk, since nobody had the bandwidth to act on a stranger's concept. What changed is the cost of acting. When a well-equipped operator can read thousands of submissions and stand up a working build in days, the old math that made sharing safe no longer holds the same way.

What should I do with an idea instead of broadcasting it?

Build the smallest version that actually works and put it in front of one real user. Say almost nothing while you do it. Then share deliberately, with people who have something at stake alongside you, rather than broadcasting into an open inbox run by someone whose incentives do not include your success. Ship first, then talk.

That is where it lands. Your experience is the one asset nobody can generate, scrape, or synthesize, because it cost you something to get. Guard it the way you would guard anything else you paid for in years. Build the small version, keep your mouth shut while you do it, and hand the finished thing to the world instead of handing the blueprint to a stranger. Let's be careful out there.

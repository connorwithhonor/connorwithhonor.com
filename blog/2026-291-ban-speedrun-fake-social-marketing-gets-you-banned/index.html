How Fake Social Marketing Gets You Banned The Ban Speedrun

October 16, 2026 // Daily Download // Connor MacIvor
TL;DR I read the platform rulebooks backwards and built the fastest way off the internet. Eight moves: buy followers, write adults only on a listing ad, post somebody else's listing photos, leave off your license number, use a hit song, send 400 DMs before lunch, buy reviews, and promise something you can't prove. Every one of them is in the actual rules at Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Meta, the Federal Trade Commission, the Fair Housing Act, or CRMLS. Then look at the list again. Every item is somebody trying to look bigger than they are. The platforms aren't banning people for being small. They're banning people for faking it. The opposite of the speedrun is boring, and boring is still here next year.

The Ban Speedrun

Today we're going to get you banned. Not a little dip in your views. Not shadow banned. Gone. Account disabled, channel terminated, and maybe a letter from a federal agency for dessert.

Here's the trick. Every move below is real. It's all in the rule books. I just read them backwards. So if you sell houses, fix air conditioners, clean teeth, or run your whole business from a phone and a logo, grab a pen.

Does buying followers get you banned?

It can. Instagram removes the fake activity, limits parts of the account, and says that if you keep using those follower apps the account can be disabled or terminated. TikTok goes further. It strips fake likes and followers, can restrict or ban the account, and can ban the new accounts you make after.

The classic move goes like this. 10,000 followers for 19 dollars. Delivered by morning. You wake up famous.

And that's the cheap part. Since October 21, 2024, the Federal Trade Commission has a rule against fake reviews and testimonials, and it also reaches a business that buys fake followers it knew or should have known were fake, to look bigger than it is. Knowing violations can draw civil penalties. The FTC's published maximum is 53,088 dollars per violation.

So for 19 dollars you bought 10,000 bots. They never buy a house. They never book a cleaning. They never call you back. That's the worst 19 dollars you'll ever spend.

Can a listing ad that says "adults only" break the law?

Yes. The Fair Housing Act makes it illegal to advertise a preference based on familial status. Two words on a regular three bedroom on a regular street, "adults only," does exactly that. The exception is housing that actually qualifies as housing for older persons, the 55 and over or 62 and over communities. A regular house is not one of them.

That's not against the rules of the app. That's federal law.

I did 20 years at LAPD. I know the difference between a speeding ticket and a felony stop. Lights, spotlight, both hands where we can see them. This one is the felony stop, and the platform is the least of your problems.

And the robots are paying attention too. In 2022, Meta settled with the Department of Justice over how housing ads were being delivered. Today a housing ad on Facebook goes in a special ad category that limits who you can target. Age, gender and ZIP code targeting are limited or gone. Meta warns advertisers that ads can be rejected when the right category is not chosen. So the government watches the words, and the robots watch the targeting.

Can you use another agent's listing photos?

No. A photographer owns those photos the moment they're taken, so posting them as yours can be copyright infringement. And in our multiple listing service, copying photos from another agent's listing breaks a specific CRMLS rule, 11.5(d).

Here's the speedrun version. Find a gorgeous listing that isn't yours. Right click. Save every photo. Post them like you shot them at sunrise.

While you're at it, leave your license number off. California wants your name, your license number, and your responsible broker on first contact advertising. Skip all three and you've built yourself a three for one special.

Real estate has its own version of every shortcut. If you want the long view on how agents get caught, read they're using AI to catch me lying. Good. Bring it.

What happens if you use a hit song in your video?

On YouTube, a Content ID claim usually hits only that video. A takedown request is different. That's a copyright strike, and three strikes in 90 days can end the channel. Facebook and Instagram can block, mute or remove the video, and can disable accounts that keep infringing.

Not the free library. The number one song in the country. The one whose label keeps lawyers on speed dial.

Here's the funny part. Instagram's popular music library is meant for personal use, so some business accounts don't even get access to it. Meta points brands to its Sound Collection, more than 14,000 royalty free songs and sounds you can use for commercial work. The app is trying to save you from yourself. Let it.

Is mass DMing strangers against Instagram rules?

It gets you warned, then blocked. Instagram says that if you're warned about sending too many messages and keep going, you may not be able to send direct messages for a period of time. And there's a hard ceiling on following. Nobody can follow more than 7,500 accounts.

The speedrun move: open your DMs and send "Hey friend" to 400 strangers before lunch. Hit the ceiling and you're the guy at the open house nobody will let past the front door.

Are bought reviews illegal?

Fake reviews and testimonials are. Same FTC rule as the fake followers. Reviews written for people who never did business with you, testimonials from people who don't exist. It's all in there, with the same penalties attached.

You want to look popular, so you buy five stars, glowing, from strangers. You just paid to put yourself inside a federal rule.

What happens when you promise results you can't prove?

This is the grand finale. The one that gets you a warning, then a strike, then the door.

"Work with me and you'll make 50,000 dollars a month." That's my example of a bad claim, not a promise from me. The FTC treats an income claim you can't back up as deceptive, and a "results not typical" line at the bottom doesn't fix it. Meta bans get rich quick ads.

"That one trick cures back pain forever." YouTube removes medical claims that contradict health authority guidance, like a guaranteed cure. The first one is usually a warning. Then come the strikes, and three in 90 days can end the channel. TikTok can remove harmful medical misinformation, take it out of the For You feed, and ban accounts that keep doing it.

Pick your poison.

The whole list

Fake followers. Adults only. Somebody else's photos. No license number. A hit song. 400 DMs. Bought reviews. A promise you can't keep. Run that whole list and you could be gone by Friday, and it's still only Tuesday.

The platforms aren't banning people for being small. They're banning people for faking it.

Why does boring marketing win?

Look at that list one more time. Every single item is somebody trying to look bigger, faster and louder than they really are. And with AI, you can look like the king of the world by lunch. That's exactly the trap. I wrote about the same instinct in why AI avatar videos can hurt real estate agents and in AI lead gen offers, mostly nonsense. The shortcut that makes you look bigger is the one that makes people trust you less.

The opposite of the speedrun is almost boring:

The Boring List
  1. Photos you took, or photos you have written permission to use.
  2. Reviews from people you actually helped.
  3. Your name, license number and responsible broker right where they belong.
  4. Music you're allowed to use.
  5. Words that welcome everybody.
  6. Promises you can prove.

Boring wins. Boring is still here next year. The fast way off the internet is pretending. The slowest, richest way to stay on it is being exactly who you are, on purpose, every single day. If you want the AI version of that same idea, start with AI writing still needs your voice and your judgment and why AI only recommends one name.

Boring is also how you build a name nobody can pry out of your market. That was the whole message of the talk I previewed in dig in like an Alabama tick. You don't get there in a weekend. You get there by showing up as yourself, over and over, until people stop needing to check.

Three more moves that almost made the list

The video had 8 offenses because the clock ran out, not because the rulebooks did. Here are 3 more I read while building it. None of them made the cut on camera, and every one of them is in the actual policies.

Pretend to be somebody else. Set up a page that looks like a bigger brand, a well known agent, or a competitor. YouTube, Meta and TikTok all prohibit impersonating a person or a business, and each one can remove, restrict or ban the account. Parody and fan accounts get room when they're clearly labeled. "I thought it would be funny" is not a label.

Beg for comments. "Comment YES if you want the list." "Tag 3 friends." Facebook calls that engagement bait and demotes it, which means fewer people see the post. That's a reach penalty, not a ban. There's a bigger version, though. Making people like, share or follow to win a prize, or to unlock something exclusive, falls under Meta's spam rules, and spam can get the account restricted. The giveaway that requires a like is a bigger problem than the post that asks a question.

Copy and paste yourself across a network. Build 10 pages, post the same thing on all of them, all day, so it looks like a movement. Meta and YouTube treat repetitive content and account networks built to inflate reach as spam or inauthentic behavior. The usual penalty is reduced reach and lost monetization, with removal for networks and the worst cases. One fair note: sharing your own video on your own business page and your own profile is not automatically a violation. The rules key on volume, repetition, networks and the intent to inflate. Share your work. Don't manufacture a crowd.

A worked example: one listing launch, two ways

This is a hypothetical. No real agent, no real house. Picture an agent getting a 3 bedroom ready to hit the market on a Thursday.

The speedrun version. The photographer is booked, so the agent grabs 6 photos from a similar house down the street that sold last spring. The caption says "Perfect for a quiet couple, adults only feel." A trending song plays under it. The bio has no license number because it "looked cluttered." A tool fires off a message to everybody who liked a competitor's post. Under the listing, 5 glowing reviews appear overnight from accounts with no profile pictures. The headline promises the house "will sell in a weekend." Count it up. That's 7 of the 8 offenses on one listing, before lunch on launch day.

The boring version. The agent waits for their own photographer, or uses photos with written permission from whoever holds the rights. The CRMLS photo guidance tells listing agents to get written permission from the rights holder before using media they didn't create, and it prohibits copying photos directly from another listing. The caption describes the house: the floor plan, the yard, the light in the kitchen, the walk to the park. It never describes who should live there. The music comes from a library licensed for business use. The agent's name, license number and responsible broker are on the post. Reviews come from people the agent actually helped, whenever those people decide to write them. And the only promise is one the agent can keep: what they'll do, when they'll do it, and how the seller will hear about it.

The boring version takes longer on Thursday. It's also the version still standing on Friday.

How do you describe a house without describing the buyer?

Write about the property, not the people. That one habit keeps most agents out of the familial status problem. The Fair Housing Act makes it illegal to advertise a preference based on familial status, and the federal regulations bar words and pictures that suggest a home is or isn't available to a group. Phrases that point at a type of household, like "adults only" or "perfect for a couple without kids," can read as exactly that kind of preference. Context matters, and I'm not going to hand you a magic word list, because I couldn't verify a current one at a primary source.

What I can hand you is the rewrite habit. Instead of "great for empty nesters," write "single story, primary suite on the ground floor." Instead of "ideal for young families," write "3 bedrooms, a fenced yard, 2 blocks from the park." The facts do the selling. The reader decides whether it fits their life. That's the "words that welcome everybody" line on the boring list, turned into a sentence you can actually write.

What does the license number rule actually require?

Your name, your license number, and your responsible broker on materials meant to be the first point of contact with a consumer. That's California Business and Professions Code 10140.6, and solicitation materials include advertising in electronic media. The DRE regulation that goes with it says the license number can't be printed smaller than the smallest type in the ad.

Here's where I stay careful. The rule doesn't name every kind of social post, and I didn't find a published DRE penalty tied to a missing number. So I won't tell you every post needs it, and I won't tell you what happens if you skip it. I'll tell you what I do. If a post asks somebody to hire me, it carries my name, my license number and my brokerage. It costs one line. It's at the bottom of this page right now.

Why does AI make the speedrun faster?

Because every fake on the list just got cheaper to produce. A model can write 50 glowing reviews in a minute. It can invent a "client" who never existed, a testimonial video with a face nobody owns, a caption promising results nobody measured. My view is that the tools aren't the villain. They just remove the friction that used to slow people down before they did something dumb.

So the same standard applies, only faster. If AI helps you write the post, you still own every claim in it. I laid out a 5 question standard for any promise in AI promises, accountability and real use: who made it, what exactly, what evidence, who benefits, and how you can check it. Run your own marketing through it before anybody else does. In real estate I take it one step further. When somebody makes a promise to my client, I ask them to put it in writing. I hold my own posts to that same rule.

A 15 minute audit of your own account

Grab a coffee and go through this once. It's not legal advice. It's the list I'd run on my own pages.

The 15 Minute Account Audit
  1. Followers. Look for a spike you can't explain, or a wave of accounts with no posts and no photos. If you ever paid for followers or engagement, stop, and stop using the app that sold them.
  2. Reviews. Every review should come from a real person who did real business with you. If you can't name the transaction, you have a problem.
  3. Photos. For every image in your last 20 posts, answer one question: who took it, and do I have written permission?
  4. License line. Check your bio and every post that asks someone to hire you for your name, license number and responsible broker.
  5. Music. On a business account, use a library built for commercial use. Meta's Sound Collection has more than 14,000 royalty free songs and sounds for exactly this.
  6. Words. Search your old listing posts for anything that describes who should live there instead of what the house is.
  7. Automation. If a tool sends messages, follows or comments for you, read the platform rules first. TikTok prohibits automation tools and scripts designed to get around its systems, and Meta's spam rules target activity at very high frequencies.
  8. Claims. Read every promise on your page out loud. If you can't prove it, rewrite it as something you'll do, not something you guarantee.

Most people find 1 or 2 problems. Almost nobody finds all 8. Fix what you find, then put a reminder on your calendar to run it again next quarter.

What does a copyright problem actually cost?

More than the photo was worth. Under federal copyright law, statutory damages run from 750 dollars to 30,000 dollars per work, and up to 150,000 dollars when the infringement is willful. Whether a photographer can claim statutory damages depends partly on when the work was registered, so not every case gets there. A right click is still not a license.

Compare that to doing it right. Book your own photographer, or get written permission from the person who holds the rights. That's the whole fix.

The limits of this list

I built this from the platform help centers, federal rules, and the CRMLS and California sources as they read in October 2026. Policies move. TikTok's current community guidelines took effect September 24, 2026, and the next version will change something somewhere. A few things I looked for and couldn't verify at a primary source, so they're not in here: a published daily limit for Instagram messages or follows beyond the 7,500 follow ceiling, a CRMLS fine schedule for photo reuse, and a specific DRE penalty for a missing license number. If somebody quotes you a number for any of those, ask where it came from.

And none of this is legal advice. If you're facing an actual complaint, a takedown, or a fair housing question, talk to a lawyer who handles that work.

Common Questions

Does buying followers get your account banned?

It can. Instagram removes fake activity from follower apps, limits the account, and says continued use can get the account disabled or terminated. TikTok removes fake likes and followers and can restrict or ban the account, including new accounts you create after.

What is the FTC fake reviews rule?

Since October 21, 2024, the Federal Trade Commission's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials bans fake reviews and testimonials, including ones from people who don't exist, and bars a business from buying fake social media influence it knew or should have known was fake. Courts can impose civil penalties for knowing violations, with a published maximum of 53,088 dollars per violation.

Is it legal to write adults only in a real estate listing ad?

Not on a regular home. The Fair Housing Act makes it illegal to advertise a preference based on familial status. The exception is housing that qualifies as housing for older persons, such as qualifying 55 and over or 62 and over communities.

Can I post another agent's listing photos on social media?

No. The photographer owns the copyright, so using the photos can be copyright infringement, and copying photos from another agent's listing breaks CRMLS Rule 11.5(d). California licensees also need their name, license number and responsible broker on first contact advertising.

Does a Content ID claim count as a YouTube copyright strike?

No. A Content ID claim usually affects only that video. A valid takedown request is a copyright strike, and three copyright strikes in 90 days makes a channel subject to termination.

Is engagement bait against Facebook's rules?

Facebook demotes posts that explicitly ask for comments, tags, shares or reactions for purposes other than a specific call to action, so fewer people see them. That is a reach penalty, not a ban. Requiring likes or shares to enter a giveaway or unlock exclusive content falls under Meta's spam rules, which can restrict the account.

Can I post the same video on more than one of my accounts?

Sharing your own work on your own pages is not automatically a violation. Meta and YouTube target repetitive content at high volume and networks of accounts built to inflate reach. The usual penalty is reduced reach and lost monetization, with removal for networks and severe cases.

Do I need my license number on social media posts?

California Business and Professions Code 10140.6 requires a licensee's name, license number and responsible broker on solicitation materials intended to be the first point of contact with consumers, including electronic media advertising. Connor puts all three on any post that asks someone to hire him. Check with your broker for your own practice.

Can I impersonate another brand or agent on social media?

No. YouTube, Meta and TikTok all prohibit impersonating a person or business, and each can remove, restrict or ban the account. Clearly labeled parody and fan accounts are treated differently.

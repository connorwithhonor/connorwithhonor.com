How Fake Social Marketing Gets You Banned The Ban Speedrun

October 16, 2026 // Daily Download // Connor MacIvor
TL;DR I read the platform rulebooks backwards and built the fastest way off the internet. Eight moves: buy followers, write adults only on a listing ad, post somebody else's listing photos, leave off your license number, use a hit song, send 400 DMs before lunch, buy reviews, and promise something you can't prove. Every one of them is in the actual rules at Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Meta, the Federal Trade Commission, the Fair Housing Act, or CRMLS. Then look at the list again. Every item is somebody trying to look bigger than they are. The platforms aren't banning people for being small. They're banning people for faking it. The opposite of the speedrun is boring, and boring is still here next year.

The Ban Speedrun

Today we're going to get you banned. Not a little dip in your views. Not shadow banned. Gone. Account disabled, channel terminated, and maybe a letter from a federal agency for dessert.

Here's the trick. Every move below is real. It's all in the rule books. I just read them backwards. So if you sell houses, fix air conditioners, clean teeth, or run your whole business from a phone and a logo, grab a pen.

Does buying followers get you banned?

It can. Instagram removes the fake activity, limits parts of the account, and says that if you keep using those follower apps the account can be disabled or terminated. TikTok goes further. It strips fake likes and followers, can restrict or ban the account, and can ban the new accounts you make after.

The classic move goes like this. 10,000 followers for 19 dollars. Delivered by morning. You wake up famous.

And that's the cheap part. Since October 21, 2024, the Federal Trade Commission has a rule against fake reviews and testimonials, and it also reaches a business that buys fake followers it knew or should have known were fake, to look bigger than it is. Knowing violations can draw civil penalties. The FTC's published maximum is 53,088 dollars per violation.

So for 19 dollars you bought 10,000 bots. They never buy a house. They never book a cleaning. They never call you back. That's the worst 19 dollars you'll ever spend.

Can a listing ad that says "adults only" break the law?

Yes. The Fair Housing Act makes it illegal to advertise a preference based on familial status. Two words on a regular three bedroom on a regular street, "adults only," does exactly that. The exception is housing that actually qualifies as housing for older persons, the 55 and over or 62 and over communities. A regular house is not one of them.

That's not against the rules of the app. That's federal law.

I did 20 years at LAPD. I know the difference between a speeding ticket and a felony stop. Lights, spotlight, both hands where we can see them. This one is the felony stop, and the platform is the least of your problems.

And the robots are paying attention too. In 2022, Meta settled with the Department of Justice over how housing ads were being delivered. Today a housing ad on Facebook goes in a special ad category that limits who you can target. Age, gender and ZIP code targeting are limited or gone. Meta warns advertisers that ads can be rejected when the right category is not chosen. So the government watches the words, and the robots watch the targeting.

Can you use another agent's listing photos?

No. A photographer owns those photos the moment they're taken, so posting them as yours can be copyright infringement. And in our multiple listing service, copying photos from another agent's listing breaks a specific CRMLS rule, 11.5(d).

Here's the speedrun version. Find a gorgeous listing that isn't yours. Right click. Save every photo. Post them like you shot them at sunrise.

While you're at it, leave your license number off. California wants your name, your license number, and your responsible broker on first contact advertising. Skip all three and you've built yourself a three for one special.

Real estate has its own version of every shortcut. If you want the long view on how agents get caught, read they're using AI to catch me lying. Good. Bring it.

What happens if you use a hit song in your video?

On YouTube, a Content ID claim usually hits only that video. A takedown request is different. That's a copyright strike, and three strikes in 90 days can end the channel. Facebook and Instagram can block, mute or remove the video, and can disable accounts that keep infringing.

Not the free library. The number one song in the country. The one whose label keeps lawyers on speed dial.

Here's the funny part. Instagram's popular music library is meant for personal use, so some business accounts don't even get access to it. Meta points brands to its Sound Collection, more than 14,000 royalty free songs and sounds you can use for commercial work. The app is trying to save you from yourself. Let it.

Is mass DMing strangers against Instagram rules?

It gets you warned, then blocked. Instagram says that if you're warned about sending too many messages and keep going, you may not be able to send direct messages for a period of time. And there's a hard ceiling on following. Nobody can follow more than 7,500 accounts.

The speedrun move: open your DMs and send "Hey friend" to 400 strangers before lunch. Hit the ceiling and you're the guy at the open house nobody will let past the front door.

Are bought reviews illegal?

Fake reviews and testimonials are. Same FTC rule as the fake followers. Reviews written for people who never did business with you, testimonials from people who don't exist. It's all in there, with the same penalties attached.

You want to look popular, so you buy five stars, glowing, from strangers. You just paid to put yourself inside a federal rule.

What happens when you promise results you can't prove?

This is the grand finale. The one that gets you a warning, then a strike, then the door.

"Work with me and you'll make 50,000 dollars a month." That's my example of a bad claim, not a promise from me. The FTC treats an income claim you can't back up as deceptive, and a "results not typical" line at the bottom doesn't fix it. Meta bans get rich quick ads.

"That one trick cures back pain forever." YouTube removes medical claims that contradict health authority guidance, like a guaranteed cure. The first one is usually a warning. Then come the strikes, and three in 90 days can end the channel. TikTok can remove harmful medical misinformation, take it out of the For You feed, and ban accounts that keep doing it.

Pick your poison.

The whole list

Fake followers. Adults only. Somebody else's photos. No license number. A hit song. 400 DMs. Bought reviews. A promise you can't keep. Run that whole list and you could be gone by Friday, and it's still only Tuesday.

The platforms aren't banning people for being small. They're banning people for faking it.

Why does boring marketing win?

Look at that list one more time. Every single item is somebody trying to look bigger, faster and louder than they really are. And with AI, you can look like the king of the world by lunch. That's exactly the trap. I wrote about the same instinct in why AI avatar videos can hurt real estate agents and in AI lead gen offers, mostly nonsense. The shortcut that makes you look bigger is the one that makes people trust you less.

The opposite of the speedrun is almost boring:

The Boring List
  1. Photos you took, or photos you have written permission to use.
  2. Reviews from people you actually helped.
  3. Your name, license number and responsible broker right where they belong.
  4. Music you're allowed to use.
  5. Words that welcome everybody.
  6. Promises you can prove.

Boring wins. Boring is still here next year. The fast way off the internet is pretending. The slowest, richest way to stay on it is being exactly who you are, on purpose, every single day. Common Questions

Does buying followers get your account banned?

It can. Instagram removes fake activity from follower apps, limits the account, and says continued use can get the account disabled or terminated. TikTok removes fake likes and followers and can restrict or ban the account, including new accounts you create after.

What is the FTC fake reviews rule?

Since October 21, 2024, the Federal Trade Commission's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials bans fake reviews and testimonials, including ones from people who don't exist, and bars a business from buying fake social media influence it knew or should have known was fake. Courts can impose civil penalties for knowing violations, with a published maximum of 53,088 dollars per violation.

Is it legal to write adults only in a real estate listing ad?

Not on a regular home. The Fair Housing Act makes it illegal to advertise a preference based on familial status. The exception is housing that qualifies as housing for older persons, such as qualifying 55 and over or 62 and over communities.

Can I post another agent's listing photos on social media?

No. The photographer owns the copyright, so using the photos can be copyright infringement, and copying photos from another agent's listing breaks CRMLS Rule 11.5(d). California licensees also need their name, license number and responsible broker on first contact advertising.

Does a Content ID claim count as a YouTube copyright strike?

No. A Content ID claim usually affects only that video. A valid takedown request is a copyright strike, and three copyright strikes in 90 days makes a channel subject to termination.
